Shower plumbing with PEX: a Melrose, MA homeowner guide

PEX is a flexible plastic pipe that many plumbers now use for shower supply lines. Copper is the older standard and still works well. Either one is fine behind tile if it is installed correctly, tested under pressure and inspected before the wall is closed.

In Melrose, plumbing work on a bathroom remodel goes through the city's permit process, and the city publishes fees for remodel fixtures. Ask Inspectional Services what your scope needs. This guide covers pipe choice, valve types, the sequence of a shower rebuild and the permit facts the city publishes.

PEX or copper in the shower wall

Both materials are widely used when installed to the manufacturer's instructions. The choice comes down to the house and the job.

PEX

  • Flexible, so fewer fittings behind the wall
  • Resists bursting from a single freeze better than rigid pipe
  • Faster to install in tight remodels
  • Must be protected from sunlight and sharp edges

Copper

  • Long track record in older homes
  • Rigid, so it needs more fittings and soldering
  • Handles heat and sunlight well
  • Pairs naturally with existing copper

If your house already has copper and you are only moving the shower valve, a good plumber can transition to PEX with approved fittings. If the whole run is old galvanized steel, the conversation is about repiping, not just the shower.

Shower valve types at a glance

Valve typeWhat it doesBest for
Pressure-balancingKeeps temperature steady when a toilet flushesMost single-handle showers
ThermostaticHolds a set temperature and splits flowMulti-head and body-spray showers
DiverterSends water to tub spout or shower headTub and shower combinations
Transfer or volume-controlRuns several outlets separatelyLarger custom showers

Pick the valve before you pick trim. The rough-in valve sits in the wall, and the trim only covers it.

How a shower plumbing rebuild runs

  1. 1

    Open the wall and plan the layout

    Studs get framed for the valve, with a blocked backer for the shower arm.

  2. 2

    Rough in the supply and the drain

    The plumber sets the valve at the right depth for your tile, runs PEX or copper, and ties in the drain.

  3. 3

    Pressure test

    The supply lines are tested with air or water, then watched for a drop. Do not skip this.

  4. 4

    Rough-in inspection

    The inspector sees the pipes before the wall is closed.

  5. 5

    Close the wall and finish

    Backer board, waterproofing and tile go on, then the trim.

Closing a wall before inspection is the most expensive mistake in a remodel. Ask your plumber to hold the schedule for the inspector.

Melrose note: permits, fees and inspection days

Melrose Inspectional Services issues plumbing and gas permits. You apply online through the city's permit portal, and the city says staff review can take 2 to 3 weeks depending on volume. The printed permit must be posted at the job site.

Published fees include a $15 administration fee per application, a $30 residential minimum, and $75 for the first four fixtures in a remodel with $10 for each additional fixture. A reinspection costs $50, and work started without a permit is charged triple the fee. Fees can change, so confirm them with the city.

Plumbing inspections run Monday to Thursday, with none on Friday, and are scheduled by phone during a one-hour window at midday on the day you want. Sub-trade permits go only to licensed professionals. Melrose also states that the 10th Edition of the Massachusetts State Building Code took full effect on January 1, 2025. That is the building code, and the plumbing code is a separate set of rules your licensed plumber follows.

Why the rough-in test matters

A shower wall hides its plumbing for decades. If a fitting is weak, the leak will rot framing and ruin tile before anyone sees a wet spot. The pressure test catches that when fixing it takes minutes instead of a demolition.

Ask to watch the test or at least see the gauge. A stable gauge over a set period means the lines hold. A falling gauge means a bad crimp or joint, and that needs fixing before the inspector arrives.

If you are planning bathroom work in another town, check with that town's building office for its permit and inspection steps. We also serve Stoneham and Wakefield.

Waterproofing and the wall behind the valve

Plumbing in a shower wall sits behind a waterproofing layer, and the valve has to line up with it. If the rough-in valve sits too deep or too shallow, the trim does not seat and leaks into the wall. A good plumber measures from the finished wall surface, not the stud.

Valve placement also affects usability. Most people set the valve about waist height so it can be reached without stepping under the spray. The shower arm goes higher and needs solid blocking, because a loose arm pulls on the pipe over time.

Ask about access. A panel on the other side of the wall, such as in a closet or hallway, makes later valve repairs far easier than cutting through tile.

PEX details that matter

PEX connections use crimp rings, clamp rings or push-fit fittings. Each system has its own tool and its own rules. Mixing parts from different systems is a common source of leaks, so ask your plumber which system is going in.

Support matters too. PEX needs clips or straps at regular intervals so it does not sag or rub against framing. Where it passes through studs, protective sleeves or plates stop nails and screws from hitting it later.

Keep a photo record before the wall closes. A picture of the pipe layout helps anyone who drills or repairs in that wall years later.

Hot water and shower performance

A new shower often exposes old problems. Low flow at the head can come from a partly closed shutoff, a clogged cartridge or undersized supply pipe. If the new valve is not the issue, the pipes feeding it may be.

Check the water heater too. A body-spray shower can draw hot water faster than a small tank can supply, and the temperature drops partway through. Talk through the fixtures you plan to run at once before you order parts.

Finally, ask about shutoff stops at the shower. A service stop on each supply makes later cartridge changes far less messy.
