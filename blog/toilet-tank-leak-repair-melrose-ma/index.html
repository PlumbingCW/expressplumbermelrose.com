Toilet tank leak repair in Melrose, MA

Most toilet tank leaks come from a worn flapper, a fill valve that never shuts off, or loose tank-to-bowl bolts. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait 15 minutes without flushing, and look in the bowl. Color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking. Water on the floor under the tank points to the bolts, the supply line or the tank itself.

The silent kind is the costly kind. A toilet that trickles into the bowl can waste water for weeks before anyone hears it.

The dye test, step by step

  1. 1

    Remove the lid

    Set it somewhere safe. Look for water running over the overflow tube.

  2. 2

    Add color

    Drop in a few drops of food coloring or a dye tablet. Do not flush.

  3. 3

    Wait 15 minutes

    Leave the toilet alone. Do not use it.

  4. 4

    Check the bowl

    If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper or flush valve seat is leaking.

  5. 5

    Flush and wipe

    Flush, dry around the tank and bolts, then watch for new drips.

If the bowl stays clear but the floor is wet, skip to the table below.

Fix the cheap parts first. A flapper or fill valve costs a small amount compared with the water a leak wastes.

Parts that fail first

Flappers are rubber and soften with age. A flapper that does not seal lets water slip into the bowl a little at a time, which makes the fill valve refill the tank over and over. You may hear the toilet turn on by itself.

Fill valves wear internally. If the float is set correctly and the tank still refills nonstop, the valve is done. Replacement kits are inexpensive, and a plumber can swap one quickly.

Tank bolts are the other regular culprit. The rubber washers harden, then water seeps along the bolt after each flush. Tighten gently, a quarter turn at a time, because porcelain cracks if you overdo it.

Melrose note: billing and selling a house

Melrose bills for water quarterly. A toilet leaking into the bowl does not show on a bill until the quarter closes, so you can run up months of waste before you see the number. Run the dye test whenever a bill jumps, or twice a year as a habit.

If you plan to sell, the city asks owners to schedule a final water reading, ideally at least 7 days before closing. Staff must get into the property to read and inspect the meter, and a $25 meter read fee is added to the final water bill. Fix a leaking toilet before that day, so the final reading is not inflated by a silent leak.

When it is not the toilet

If the dye test is clean and the meter still moves with everything off, the leak is elsewhere. A supply line, a hidden pipe or an irrigation line could be responsible. In that case leak detection can trace it without opening walls.

How to replace a flapper

Shut the supply valve at the wall and flush to empty the tank. Unhook the old flapper from the ears on the overflow tube and the chain from the handle arm. Take it with you to the store, because flappers come in more than one size and shape.

Clip the new flapper on, attach the chain with a little slack, and turn the water back on. Run the dye test again. If color still shows in the bowl, wipe the flush valve seat with a cloth, because mineral film or a nick in the seat can stop a new flapper from sealing.

A chain that is too tight holds the flapper open a crack, and one that is too long can slip under it. Aim for about half an inch of slack.

What a silent leak costs on a quarterly bill

A silent leak has no sound and no puddle, so it runs until someone reads a bill. With a quarterly cycle, one missed leak can cover a full billing period before you learn about it. The number on the bill is the first sign, not the first day of the problem.

Compare your bill to the same quarter last year. If usage is up and nothing changed in the household, do the dye test and the meter check. Fixing a flapper takes minutes, and finding it early keeps the next bill from repeating the surprise.

If you rent out a unit, ask tenants to report running toilets quickly. A quick note costs nothing, and a quarter of waste does.

Fill valve and float adjustments

If the tank refills too high, water spills into the overflow tube and the fill valve hisses. Most valves have a float cup or a screw you can turn to lower the water level. The water line should sit about an inch below the top of the overflow tube.

Adjust a little at a time and flush between changes. If the valve still will not shut off, replace it. The kits fit most standard tanks, and the swap takes a short visit.
