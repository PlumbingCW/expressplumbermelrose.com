Replacing a water heater: copper pipe, flex connectors and what Melrose, MA homes need

For most replacements, either hard copper or listed stainless flex connectors work at the water heater, and the better choice depends on space, the age of your piping and your plumber's preference. Copper lasts longer. Flex connectors install faster and forgive a tank that sits a half inch off. PEX is normally used on the wall side with a short metal stub at the tank.

Follow the manufacturer's instructions and the Massachusetts plumbing code through a licensed plumber to decide what is allowed on your job. Here is how the options compare, where dielectric fittings fit in, and what the Melrose permit and inspection steps look like.

Copper pipe versus flex connectors

These are the two connections you will hear about most often when a water heater is swapped.

Hard copper

  • Long service life when joined correctly
  • Rigid, so the tank must line up with the piping
  • Joined by solder or press fittings, both need skill
  • Neat result that handles heat well

Braided stainless flex connectors

  • Fast to install and tolerant of small misalignment
  • Must be listed for potable water and rated for the temperature
  • Shorter life than copper, and kinks restrict flow
  • Good fit for tight spaces or where the tank may need to move

Neither is wrong. A careful installer chooses based on the room, not habit.

Where PEX fits, and where it does not

PEX tubing is flexible and common in new work, and it is easy to run long distances. Many heater manufacturers do not want PEX connected straight to the tank. They call for a short section of metal pipe or a listed connector between the tank and the plastic, which protects the tubing from heat at the fittings.

If your home is piped in PEX already, expect a copper stub or flex connector at the heater. If you are thinking of changing old supply lines throughout the house, that is a different project, covered under repiping. A tank swap alone rarely justifies it, but old, corroded supply lines are worth noting while the walls are open.

Soldering is not better than pressing, or the other way around. Soldering has a long track record, and pressing removes the flame risk in a finished basement or near wood framing.

Parts that matter more than they look

Dielectric fittings

When steel heater threads meet copper pipe, the two metals can corrode each other. A dielectric union or nipple breaks that path. Many tanks now ship with them, and an installer will confirm what you have.

Shutoff valve

A full-port ball valve on the cold inlet is a small upgrade that pays off the next time something leaks. If the old valve is a stiff gate valve, replace it now.

Expansion tank

If your system is closed by a backflow or check valve, thermal expansion needs somewhere to go. An expansion tank handles that and protects the relief valve from constant cycling.

Relief valve and discharge pipe

The T&P valve and its drain line must be installed to the manual. Never cap it or reduce the pipe size.

If your plumber is working on a tank replacement and wants to talk about these four items, that is a good sign.

Melrose note: permits, inspections and your water

The City of Melrose fee schedule lists a water heater at $40 and a residential gas water heater at $40, plus a $15 administration fee per application. Work started without a permit is charged triple, a reinspection is $50, and after-hours inspections are $400 for the first 2 hours. Plumbing and gas permits are applied for online and staff review can take 2 to 3 weeks, so ask your plumber to start the application early.

Plumbing and gas inspections happen Monday through Thursday, with none on Friday, and are scheduled by phone between 12 pm and 1 pm on the day wanted. The printed permit must be posted at the job site. Permits go only to licensed professionals, so a plumber should pull it. Your water is MWRA supply, which is pH-adjusted to reduce corrosion, but that does not prevent galvanic corrosion at dissimilar metals, which is why the dielectric fitting still matters.

How a typical swap goes

  1. 1

    Shut off and drain

    The water, gas or power goes off and the tank drains to a floor drain or hose.

  2. 2

    Disconnect the old unit

    Unions, connectors, vent and the relief discharge come off. Corroded fittings are noted.

  3. 3

    Set the new heater and connect

    The tank is leveled, the connectors or copper are fitted, and dielectric fittings go in where metals meet.

  4. 4

    Fill and check

    Open a hot tap to purge air, then check every joint for weeping before powering on.

  5. 5

    Inspect and finish

    Any permit the city requires is posted and any needed inspection is booked. Gas models get a combustion and venting check.

Ask your plumber to walk you through the shutoffs before they leave. It takes two minutes and could save a flood later.

A note on doing it yourself

The city states that sub-trade permits are granted only to licensed professionals, and that an owner who hires a contractor under an owner permit may not be protected by the Home Improvement Guarantee Fund. For a gas heater especially, a licensed plumber handles the permit, the venting and the gas connection together.

If you are comparing quotes, line up the same scope: connectors, shutoff, expansion tank, permit, haul-away. For help with the whole job, see our water heater replacement page. We also serve Malden and Saugus, though permit rules are set by each municipality. We also serve those towns.
