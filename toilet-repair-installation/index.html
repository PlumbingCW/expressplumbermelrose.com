Toilet overflowing or will not stop running

An overflowing bowl and a toilet that runs all night feel like different problems, but both start with water going where it should not. A clog sends water over the rim. A worn flapper lets tank water seep into the bowl around the clock, and since Melrose bills water quarterly, the cost shows up weeks later.

If the bowl is rising, lift the tank lid and press the flapper down into its seat to stop the fill. Then close the wall valve. That buys you hours instead of minutes while the crew heads your way.

A clog that sits deeper in the house line is a different job, covered under drain cleaning.

What usually fails inside and under a toilet

Flapper and fill valve

Rubber flappers harden and stop sealing, and fill valves stick or whistle. Both are small parts, and swapping them is the fastest cure for a running tank.

Wax ring at the floor

A toilet sits on a wax ring that seals the drain flange. Water around the base, a rocking bowl or a sewer smell all point here. We lift the toilet, replace the ring and set the bowl level.

Cracked porcelain

A hairline crack in the tank or bowl can seep for weeks before it shows. Once porcelain splits, no sealant is reliable, and replacement is the honest answer.

Loose or broken hardware

Snapped closet bolts, a stripped flush lever or a failed tank gasket cause leaks and weak flushes. These are quick repairs when the porcelain is sound.

Repair the toilet or replace it

Repair makes sense when

  • The porcelain has no cracks and the bowl does not rock.
  • The problem is a flapper, fill valve, lever or gasket.
  • The toilet flushes well whenever its parts work.
  • The leak is at the supply line or the wall valve.

Replace makes sense when

  • The tank or bowl is cracked or chipped through the glaze.
  • You are fixing the same toilet a second time in a year.
  • The old unit is a high-volume model you want to retire.
  • A remodel or a taller bowl is already planned.

Many newer toilets are rated at 1.28 gallons per flush or less, below the older 1.6 gallon standard. If you replace one, pick a bowl height that suits whoever uses it every day.

How a toilet replacement visit goes

  1. 1

    Shut off and drain

    We close the wall valve, empty the tank and sponge out the bowl so nothing spills during the lift.

  2. 2

    Pull and inspect

    The old bowl comes up, the flange gets checked for cracks and rot, and the floor is cleaned of old wax.

  3. 3

    Set and seal

    A new wax ring goes down, the bowl is seated level and the closet bolts are snugged evenly so the porcelain does not crack.

  4. 4

    Connect and test

    The supply line is attached, the tank is filled and the toilet is flushed several times while we watch the base for seepage. We haul the old unit away.

Install timing depends on parts and on what the flange looks like once the old toilet is out. A rotted subfloor or a damaged flange adds work, and we tell you before we do it.

Before we arrive

1

Close the wall valve

Turn it clockwise until it stops. If it spins freely or leaks, shut the main water valve instead.

2

Stop flushing

Every flush adds water to a bowl that may already be backing up.

3

Protect the floor

Lay towels around the base and move rugs, bath mats and anything stored under the tank.

4

Note the pattern

Tell us if other drains gurgle. That points to a line problem rather than the toilet.

If sewage is coming up in a tub or floor drain too, stay out of the water and say so on the call. That is a sewer line question first.
