Does a tankless water heater need a pressure relief valve? A Melrose, MA guide

Usually yes, or at least the installation needs one somewhere. A tankless water heater holds only a small amount of water, so it behaves differently from a tank, but many manufacturers still call for a pressure relief valve. Some units ship with one built in, and others expect the plumber to add it.

The final answer for your house comes from the installation manual for your exact model. Follow the manufacturer's instructions and the Massachusetts plumbing code through a licensed plumber. This guide shows how to tell which setup you have, what to ask before work starts, and what the Melrose permit side looks like.

Why a relief valve exists at all

A relief valve is the last line of defense when pressure or temperature climbs past a safe limit. It opens, lets water out through a discharge pipe, and keeps the heater and your pipes from taking a pressure they were never built for. It has no electronics and no software, which is why it is trusted as a safety device.

On a storage tank, the temperature and pressure (T&P) valve is standard trade practice and familiar. It sits near the top of the tank and the tank holds 40 to 80 gallons that can expand and flash to steam. A tankless unit heats water only as it flows, so the stored volume is tiny. That is the reason people assume it needs no valve.

The catch is that the water pipes feeding the heater still hold pressure, and the burner or element can still overheat if a sensor fails. Manufacturers write their own protection requirements around those risks. If you are choosing a tankless water heater, the relief valve question belongs in the quote, not as a surprise on install day.

The pattern is simple. The more closed-off your plumbing is, the more a relief valve matters. A plain open system with a built-in valve is the easy case, and a closed system with a backflow device is where installers earn their keep.

Tank heater versus tankless on relief protection

People often ask whether the valve rule is just a tank thing. It is not, but the details differ.

Storage tank heater

  • Has a T&P valve that responds to both pressure and temperature
  • Discharge pipe runs down toward the floor or a drain
  • Valve is a standard part, easy to find and test
  • Large stored volume means a big consequence if protection fails

Tankless heater

  • Protection may be built in, external, or both, depending on the model
  • Manual decides where the valve goes and what it must be rated for
  • Small stored volume, but pipes upstream can still see pressure spikes
  • Gas models add venting and combustion rules on top of the water side

Either way, a manufacturer may refuse a warranty claim on an installation that skips a required part. That alone is a practical reason to read the manual before the plumber arrives.

This is also why a swap from a tank to tankless is more than a box-for-box trade. A full water heater installation covers the water side, the gas or electric supply, the vent for gas models, and the discharge route for any relief valve. Each of those has its own rules.

How to check your own tankless heater in ten minutes

  1. 1

    Find the data plate

    Look for a label on the side or bottom of the unit with the model number, serial number and maximum working pressure. Take a photo with your phone.

  2. 2

    Look for a valve and a pipe

    A relief valve has a lever or a brass body with a discharge pipe leading away. Check the water outlet piping and the cold inlet for one. Note whether the pipe ends somewhere safe.

  3. 3

    Pull up the manual

    Search the model number online and open the installation manual, not the user guide. Find the diagram showing the relief valve and the notes on closed systems.

  4. 4

    Check for a check valve or backflow device

    Look at the water meter area and main shutoff. A one-way device means the system is likely closed and needs a plan for thermal expansion.

  5. 5

    Write down your questions

    Bring the photos, model number and any code questions to the plumber. Ask who pulls the permit before you agree to the schedule.

If you find a valve with no discharge pipe, or a pipe that ends in a plugged cap, stop there. That is a sign to call a licensed plumber, not to fix it yourself.

Melrose note: water and permit fees

Melrose buys its water from MWRA, and the 2023 Melrose water report lists monochloramine as the disinfectant, with an average of 1.98 ppm. MWRA also adjusts pH and buffering with sodium carbonate and carbon dioxide to reduce corrosion.

The report gives no hardness number, so do not assume scale is a problem. If the manual asks for periodic flushing or descaling, follow its schedule, and test your tap or ask the water utility if you want a hardness reading first.

On the permit side, the city's fee schedule lists a water heater at $40, and a residential gas water heater at $40 as well. There is also a $15 administration fee per application, and the schedule charges triple for work started without a permit. Plumbing permits are applied for online and staff review can run 2 to 3 weeks depending on volume. Have your plumber tell you who is pulling the permit and when the inspection is booked.

Do not cap, plug or tamper with a relief valve

Never plug a relief valve, never cap its discharge pipe and never swap it for a plain fitting because it drips. A valve that weeps is telling you something, such as high pressure from a closed system or a worn seat. If you see one dripping, shut off the heater per the manual and have a licensed plumber look at it.

If you ever smell gas near a gas tankless heater, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 from outside before you call a plumber.
